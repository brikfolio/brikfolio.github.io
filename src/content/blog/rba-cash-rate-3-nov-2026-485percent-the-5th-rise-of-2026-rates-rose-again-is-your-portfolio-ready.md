## Rates are at an 18-year high. Is your portfolio ready for the next move?

On Melbourne Cup Day, the Reserve Bank lifted the cash rate by 0.25% to 4.85%. It was the fifth rise of 2026. Rates are now 1.25% higher than they were in January, and at their highest level since 2008.

The vote was split six to three. Some economists think the end is near. Others expect more. Nobody knows for sure. The next decision is due on 8 December.

For property investors, the useful question is not "will rates rise again?" It is "what happens to *my* portfolio if they do?" That question has a real answer. You just need the right numbers in one place.

## Three questions every investor should be able to answer

1. **How much more rate rise can my portfolio absorb?** Not just for one loan. Across every property, with rent, costs and repayments together.
2. **When does my cash run out?** If rates rise, rent drops or a property sits empty, which month does your buffer fall below where you are comfortable?
3. **Is refinancing actually worth it?** A lower rate looks good on paper. After fees, break costs and cashback conditions, does it still win? And when do you break even?

If you can answer all three today, you are ahead of most investors. If you can't, you are not alone.

## Why the spreadsheet stops helping

Most investors start with a spreadsheet. It works until the portfolio grows. Then the gaps show:

- **The data lives everywhere.** Loan statements, property manager statements, settlement papers and bank exports all sit in different places.
- **It looks backwards.** A spreadsheet tells you what happened last quarter. It rarely tells you what happens next year if the rate moves.
- **Small errors compound.** A weekly repayment entered as monthly, or rounding at every step, can throw a 10-year view out by thousands.
- **Fees get forgotten.** A refinance that "saves" money can cost money once break costs and fees are counted.

None of this is a skills problem. It is a tools problem.

Sam then tests a refinance on the larger loan, with every fee entered. If the break-even point comes later than Sam plans to hold the loan, Sam knows it isn't worth it. If it comes sooner, Sam has numbers to take to a broker.

## Your checklist before 8 December

1. Gather your latest loan statements and property manager statements.
2. Note any fixed-rate loans and when they expire.
3. Decide your cash buffer: the lowest balance you are comfortable holding.
4. Test one more rate rise, and two. Check your worst year and break point.
5. Add one bad surprise: a vacancy, a rent cut or a big repair.
6. If refinancing is on your mind, compare it with every fee included.
7. Book time with your broker or accountant, and bring the evidence PDF.
